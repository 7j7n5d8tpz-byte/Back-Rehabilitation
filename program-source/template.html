Section 1

The strategy, and why this one is different

You have had three episodes at the same level in under two years. That pattern is the single most important fact in this document, and it drives every decision in the program.

What recurrence usually means

Repeat herniations at one level are rarely caused by one bad rep. The common pattern is a mismatch between how fast symptoms settle and how fast tissue tolerance rebuilds. Pain typically resolves in weeks; the disc and the motor control around it take considerably longer. If you resume normal training and normal life loading at the point where you feel fine, you are loading a structure that is still behind. That is the trap, and it is where most people's second and third episodes come from.

The other common contributor is that rehab happens for 45 minutes in the gym and the other 23 hours are unchanged — sitting posture, how you pick things up, morning bending. Section 9 treats that as part of the program, not as an afterthought.

The five principles this program runs on

1 — Endurance before strength, strength before load

Trunk endurance, not maximal trunk strength, is what tracks with back resilience. The early phases build the ability to hold position for a long time under modest load. Heavy comes much later, and heavy is never the point.

2 — Load the hips hard, spare the spine

You can build serious muscle and functional strength with hip-dominant, torso-supported and single-leg work that puts very little shear or flexion demand on L5/S1. The program uses trap bar and elevated hinges, split squats, hip thrusts, chest-supported rows and loaded carries — all of which load the target tissue heavily while keeping your lumbar spine in neutral. Every squat, thrust and calf exercise is single-leg, so neither side can hide behind the other. The hinge stays two-legged on purpose: a symmetrical stance is what keeps the lumbar spine neutral under heavy load.

3 — Centralisation is the signal, not pain intensity

Symptoms moving toward your back and away from your leg means you are on the right track, even if back pain is unchanged. Symptoms moving down the leg means stop, regardless of how mild. This rule overrides how you feel about a given exercise.

4 — Boring, slow, and long is the fast route

The program is 24 weeks and deliberately under-ambitious in the first eight. Every previous episode bought you months. A conservative ramp that you complete beats an aggressive ramp that ends in a fourth herniation.

5 — Rebuild the nervous system, not just the muscle

An S1 nerve root that has been compressed leaves measurable deficits — calf strength, balance, and proprioception — plus a protective guarding pattern that outlasts the injury. Section 7 addresses this directly. This is the "back to 100% me" part.

What you are not doing

  • No loaded lumbar flexion. No barbell from the floor with a rounding back, no sit-ups, no weighted crunches, no Roman chair flexion, no loaded toe-touches.
  • No loaded rotation for the first 12 weeks. Anti-rotation only (Pallof press, carries).
  • No barbell back squat in Phases 1–2. Axial compression through the spine plus the depth demand is the single worst risk/reward exercise for your situation. Split squats and step-ups give you the same quads with a fraction of the spinal cost.
  • No training to failure at any point in this program. Form degrades near failure, and the form that degrades first is your lumbar spine.
  • No heavy bending or lifting in the first 60–90 minutes after waking. Discs are at their most hydrated and least tolerant of flexion first thing in the morning.

Rules of engagement

The traffic light system

Red flags — same-day medical attention, not physio
  • Numbness or altered sensation in the groin, genitals, or the area you would sit on a saddle
  • New difficulty starting or stopping urination, or loss of bladder/bowel control
  • Weakness in both legs, or progressive weakness in one
  • Foot drop — catching your toes, unable to lift the front of your foot
  • Sudden severe back pain with fever, or after a fall or trauma

Session rules

  • The rep that changes your form is the last rep. If your lower back position shifts, the set is over — even if the number on the page says three more.
  • Brace before every rep. Not a suck-in. A 360° stiffening — breathe into the belly and low ribs, then tension the whole abdominal wall as if bracing for a light punch. Hold it through the rep.
  • Warm up for five minutes, every session. Non-negotiable: 3 min walk or bike, then 10 bodyweight hip hinges, 10 glute bridges, 8 cat-camel (gentle, no end range), 10 band pull-aparts.
  • RPE 5–6 means four or more reps left in the tank. RPE 7–8 means two to three. You should never be closer than two reps to failure in this program.
  • Rest fully: 2–3 min on main lifts, 60–90 s on accessories. Rushing rest is how form degrades.
  • If an exercise reproduces leg symptoms, remove it. Not "push through it." Substitutions are in Section 6.
  • Single-leg work: weaker leg first. The weaker leg sets the load and the reps; the stronger leg does the same, never more. Until the single-leg strength gap (Section 7) is under 10%, the weaker leg may do one extra set.
  • Supersets are marked SS1, SS2… (Phase 3). Do one set of each, then rest 60–90 s. The pairs were chosen to fit the layout of your gym. If it is busy and you lose one station, do them as straight sets.

Section 6

Progression, deloads and substitutions

How to add weight — double progression

Never add weight because a week has passed. Add weight when the current weight has been earned.

  1. Start at the bottom of the prescribed rep range with a weight at the prescribed RPE.
  2. Each week, add reps until you hit the top of the range on every set, at the same RPE.
  3. Then add load — 2.5–5 kg on lower-body lifts, 1–2.5 kg on upper-body — and drop back to the bottom of the range.
  4. Repeat. Expect this cycle to take 2–4 weeks per lift. That is normal and correct.

The one progression rule that matters most. Only progress a lift if the previous week was Green. An Amber week freezes all loads. This single rule is the difference between this program and whatever you were doing before March 2026.

Deloads

Take a scheduled deload in weeks {{DELOADS}} — and additionally any time you have three or more Amber days in a fortnight. Phase 1 is light enough that it does not need one. On a deload week: keep the same exercises, cut working sets by half, and cap everything at RPE 5. Do not skip these. They are when tissue actually adapts, and they are cheap insurance against the accumulation pattern that produces recurrences.

Section 7

The daily 15 minutes at home

Do this every day, including gym days. Consistency here matters more than anything in the gym — it is what keeps the motor control you build from leaking away between sessions. If a day is chaotic, do the first four items and skip the rest.

{{HOME_TABLE}}

Walking — the highest-value thing you can do

20–30 minutes daily, at a brisk pace, arms swinging, separate from the routine above.

Walking loads and unloads the disc rhythmically, pumps fluid through it, and is the single most consistently useful intervention for disc-related back pain. Brisk matters — a slow shuffle does not have the same effect. On a Red day, walking is often the only thing you should do, and you should still do it.

Nervous system work — getting back to 100%

An L5/S1 herniation compressing the S1 nerve root leaves deficits that do not resolve just because the pain did. Three of them are worth targeting deliberately.

1. Calf strength — the S1 signature

S1 supplies your calf. The classic residual deficit after an S1 root problem is a weaker calf on the affected side, and most people never notice because two-legged calf raises hide it.

Benchmark: single-leg calf raise test

Stand on one leg, hand on a wall for balance only, and do full-range calf raises to failure. Test both sides. Target: 25+ reps, and less than 20% difference between sides. Test on day 1, then every four weeks. Record it. This is the most objective marker of nerve recovery you can measure yourself, and watching it improve is worth more for your confidence than any amount of reassurance.

3. Desensitisation and graded exposure

After three episodes, some degree of movement fear is entirely rational — and also now part of the problem. A back that is guarded all day is a stiff back that distributes load badly. The mechanism is well described: the more you avoid a movement, the more threatening your nervous system rates it, and the less you do it, the less tolerance you have for it.

The countermeasure is graded exposure, not avoidance and not bravado:

  • Pick one movement you avoid — bending to tie shoes, lifting a bag from the floor, rotating to reach the back seat of the car.
  • Do a small, controlled, unloaded version of it daily. Five reps. Nothing heroic.
  • Each week, add slightly more range or slightly more load.
  • Understand that some discomfort during graded exposure is expected and is not tissue damage. The rule that decides whether to continue is still centralisation vs. peripheralisation, not intensity.

Your spine is designed to bend. The long-term goal is not a back you protect forever — it is a back that tolerates ordinary life without you thinking about it. That takes deliberate, gradual re-exposure.

Section 8

The flare-up protocol

You will have a flare at some point in 24 weeks. Having a written plan for it is the difference between losing a week and losing a season — and it stops you making the panic decision to stop moving entirely, which reliably makes things worse.

{{FLARE_STAGES}}

The debrief — do this every time. Within a week of any flare, write down: what you did in the 72 hours before it; whether the gym or daily life was the more likely cause; and what specifically you will change. Three episodes without a written debrief means the pattern that caused them is still invisible to you. This takes ten minutes and is probably the highest-leverage thing in this entire document.

Section 9

The other 23 hours

Your training is roughly 3 hours a week. Your life is 165. If the daily loading is not addressed, a good program cannot outrun it — and for recurrent herniations, daily life is more often the culprit than the gym.

Mornings

Your discs absorb fluid overnight and are at their most pressurised and least tolerant of bending for roughly the first hour after you get up. This is when a large share of disc injuries actually happen, doing something trivial.

  • No heavy bending or lifting for 60–90 minutes after waking. Not the laundry basket, not the child, not the toolbox.
  • Get out of bed by rolling to your side, then pushing up with your arms — do not sit straight up.
  • Brush your teeth standing tall, or with one foot up on the toilet seat or a small step. Do not lean over the sink with a rounded back.
  • Do not train first thing in the morning. Mid-morning or later is meaningfully safer for you.

Sitting

  • Break sitting every 30 minutes. Stand up, walk 60 seconds. Set a timer if needed — this is the single highest-return habit change on this page.
  • Sit fully back in the chair with support behind your lower back. A rolled towel works as well as an expensive cushion.
  • Screen at eye height, feet flat on the floor.
  • Long drives: stop every hour and walk. Use lumbar support — car seats are almost universally shaped to put you in flexion.

Lifting anything, ever

  • Hinge, don't round. Hips back, chest forward, spine neutral. This applies to a 40 kg trap bar and to a dropped sock equally — arguably more to the sock, because that is when you are not paying attention.
  • Get close to the object before you lift it. The further from your body, the greater the lever arm on your spine.
  • Never lift and twist in the same movement. Turn with your feet.
  • Split stance or half-kneel for low objects — one foot forward, or a knee down.

Sleep

  • Side-lying with a pillow between your knees, or on your back with a pillow under your knees.
  • Avoid sleeping face down if it stiffens you up, though for some people with an extension preference this is comfortable — go by how you feel in the morning.
  • A very soft mattress that lets your hips sink allows sustained flexion for eight hours. Medium-firm is generally the better bet.

Body composition and general health

Two things reliably influence disc health and are worth naming: smoking, which measurably impairs disc nutrition and is associated with faster degeneration, and excess abdominal mass, which increases the sustained load on the lumbar spine. Neither may apply to you — but if either does, they are worth more attention than any exercise selection detail in this document. Adequate protein (roughly 1.6–2.2 g per kg bodyweight per day) and adequate sleep also directly affect how well you adapt to the training above.

What "back to 100%" realistically looks like

By the end of week 24, a reasonable expectation is: no leg symptoms in normal life, symmetrical calf strength, the ability to pick things up off the floor without thinking about it, meaningfully more muscle than you have now, and a trap bar deadlift and hip thrust that are genuinely strong. What is not on the list is a one-rep-max back squat or a floor deadlift PR — and by design. Those specific lifts are where your risk lives, and they are not required for any of the outcomes you said you want.

Questions for your physio

Bring this document to your next appointment and specifically ask:

  • Do I have a directional preference? Does extension centralise my symptoms, or does flexion?
  • Is there any measurable S1 motor deficit right now — calf strength, ankle reflex, sensation on the outside of the foot?
  • Given three episodes in two years, is a repeat scan or a surgical opinion warranted, or is continued conservative management still the right call?
  • Is a loaded hip hinge appropriate for me at week 5, and do you want the trap bar higher than I have planned?
  • Watch my hinge and squat and tell me what you see — can I have a video review at week 4?
  • Are there specific movements you want me to avoid that are not on my list?
Closing

A note on how to hold all this

The temptation after three episodes is to train timidly forever, or to decide the back is broken and train around it. Neither works. Discs tolerate load — they need it. What they do not tolerate is load that arrives faster than adaptation, and load applied through a spine that is bent when it should be braced. This program is built around those two facts, and almost everything else in it follows from them.
